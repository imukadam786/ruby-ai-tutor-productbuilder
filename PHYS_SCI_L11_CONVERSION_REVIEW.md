## L11.A3 — Intermolecular forces & physical properties

**L11.T1.A3.q02** · ERR_CHAIN_LENGTH_BP, ERR_IMF_VS_BP  
Q: Compare the boiling points of methane (CH₄) and pentane (C₅H₁₂). Which is higher and why?  
- ✅ Pentane — larger, with more electrons and surface contact, so stronger London forces  
- ✗ Methane — smaller molecules have higher boiling points  
- ✗ Equal — both are alkanes with the same intermolecular forces  

**L11.T1.A3.q03** · ERR_BRANCHING_BP, ERR_IMF_VS_BP  
Q: Compare the boiling points of n-pentane (straight chain) and 2,2-dimethylpropane (branched, same molecular formula C₅H₁₂). Which is higher and why?  
- ✗ 2,2-dimethylpropane — branching gives stronger London forces  
- ✅ n-Pentane — its straight chain has more surface contact, so stronger London forces  
- ✗ Equal — the same molecular formula means the same boiling point  

**L11.T1.A3.q04** · ERR_HBONDING_RECOGNITION, ERR_IMF_RANKING  
Q: Compare the boiling points of butane (C₄H₁₀) and propan-1-ol (C₃H₇-OH). Which has the higher BP? Explain.  
- ✗ Butane — it has more carbon atoms, so a higher boiling point  
- ✗ Equal — both molecules have similar London forces  
- ✅ Propan-1-ol — its -OH allows hydrogen bonding, a much stronger IMF than London  

**L11.T1.A3.q05** · ERR_IMF_RANKING, ERR_IMF_VS_BP  
Q: Arrange the following in order of INCREASING boiling point: ethane (CH₃CH₃), ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH), ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH).  
- ✅ Ethane < ethanol < ethanoic acid  
- ✗ Ethanoic acid < ethanol < ethane  
- ✗ Ethanol < ethanoic acid < ethane  

**L11.T1.A3.q07** · ERR_POSITIONAL_BP, ERR_HBONDING_RECOGNITION  
Q: Why does pentan-1-ol have a higher boiling point than pentan-3-ol (both C₅H₁₂O)?  
- ✗ Pentan-3-ol — a central -OH bonds more strongly  
- ✅ Pentan-1-ol — its terminal -OH lets H-bonding form more easily (less steric hindrance)  
- ✗ Equal — same formula and functional group means the same boiling point  

**L11.T1.A3.q08** · ERR_CHAIN_LENGTH_VISCOSITY, ERR_IMF_VS_VISCOSITY  
Q: How does INCREASING CHAIN LENGTH affect the viscosity of liquid alkanes?  
- ✗ Viscosity decreases — longer molecules flow more easily  
- ✗ Chain length has no effect on viscosity  
- ✅ Viscosity increases — longer chains have stronger London forces, so molecules slide past each other less easily  

**L11.T1.A3.q11** · ERR_BRANCHING_BP  
Q: A learner says: 'Branched alkanes have stronger London forces than straight-chain ones with the same formula.' Is this correct?  
- ✅ Incorrect — branched molecules have less surface contact, so weaker London forces  
- ✗ Correct — branching increases London forces  
- ✗ Correct — branched molecules are heavier, so stronger forces  

**L11.T1.A3.q13** · ERR_HBONDING_RECOGNITION  
Q: Why is the boiling point of butan-1-ol (~118°C) so much higher than butane (~−0.5°C), despite both having 4 carbons?  
- ✗ Butane — they have the same number of carbons, so the same boiling point  
- ✅ Butan-1-ol — its -OH enables hydrogen bonding, far stronger than butane's London forces  
- ✗ Butane — being a gas, it has stronger forces than the liquid alcohol  

**L11.T1.A3.q14** · ERR_CHAIN_LENGTH_BP, ERR_CHAIN_LENGTH_VISCOSITY  
Q: Two alcohols A (chain length 4) and B (chain length 8) are compared. Which has higher boiling point and viscosity?  
- ✗ A (4 carbons) — shorter chains boil higher and are more viscous  
- ✗ Same boiling point, but B is more viscous  
- ✅ B (8 carbons) — longer chains have stronger London forces, raising both boiling point and viscosity  

**L11.T1.A3.q16** · ERR_CHAIN_LENGTH_BP, ERR_VAPOUR_PRESSURE_IMF  
Q: Why is petrol (a mixture of short-chain alkanes) more volatile than diesel (longer-chain alkanes)?  
- ✅ Petrol — shorter chains have weaker London forces, so it vaporises more easily  
- ✗ Diesel — longer chains evaporate more easily  
- ✗ Equal — both are mixtures of alkanes  

**L11.T1.A3.q17** · ERR_IMF_RANKING  
Q: List the THREE types of intermolecular forces present in a typical organic liquid, in order from STRONGEST to WEAKEST.  
- ✗ London > dipole-dipole > hydrogen bonding  
- ✅ Hydrogen bonding > dipole-dipole > London (dispersion) forces  
- ✗ Dipole-dipole > hydrogen bonding > London  

**L11.T1.A3.q18** · ERR_IMF_RANKING, ERR_HBONDING_RECOGNITION  
Q: Two compounds: P (propan-2-ol) and Q (propanone, CH₃-CO-CH₃). They are functional isomers (same C₃H₆O for Q but different functional groups). Which has the higher boiling point and why?  
- ✗ Propanone — its polar C=O gives it the stronger forces  
- ✗ Equal — they have the same molecular formula  
- ✅ Propan-2-ol — its -OH allows hydrogen bonding; propanone has only dipole-dipole and London  

**L11.T1.A3.q19** · ERR_HBONDING_RECOGNITION  
Q: Why does water have an unusually high boiling point compared to other small molecules (like CH₄ or H₂S)?  
- ✅ Water forms an extensive network of hydrogen bonds (up to four per molecule)  
- ✗ Water molecules are unusually heavy  
- ✗ Water has exceptionally strong London (dispersion) forces  

**L11.T1.A3.q20** · ERR_IMF_RANKING, ERR_IMF_VS_BP
